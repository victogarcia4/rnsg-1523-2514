FETAL DEVELOPMENT TIMELINE

FETAL DEVELOPMENT OVERVIEW

Pregnancy ≈ 280 days (40 weeks) from LMP 266 days from fertilization

Development: cephalocaudal, proximal-distal, general-to-specific.

Three stages: → Preembryonic → Embryonic → Fetal

Open speaker notes

"Pregnancy lasts approximately 280 days, or 40 weeks, from the first day of the last menstrual period. From fertilization, it is 266 days. Development proceeds cephalocaudal — that is head-to-toe — proximal to distal, and general to specific. There are three stages: preembryonic, embryonic, and fetal. We will go through each one."

PREEMBRYONIC STAGE

PREEMBRYONIC STAGE Fertilization → end of 2nd week

Fertilization: outer third of fallopian tube. Zygote → morula (16 cells, 72h) → blastocyst.

Implantation: 7–10 days after conception, upper uterus (fundus).

Three germ layers: → Ectoderm → CNS, skin, glands → Mesoderm → skeletal, circulatory → Endoderm → respiratory, liver, GI

Open speaker notes

"The preembryonic stage runs from fertilization to the end of the second week. Fertilization occurs in the outer third of the fallopian tube. The fertilized ovum is called a zygote with 46 chromosomes. Through cleavage, it becomes a morula — sixteen cells at about 72 hours — and then a blastocyst. The trophoblast, the outer layer, becomes the placenta and chorion. The inner cell mass becomes the embryo and amnion. Implantation occurs seven to ten days after conception in the upper uterus, the fundus. Three germ layers form: ectoderm gives rise to the central nervous system, skin, and glands. Mesoderm forms skeletal, circulatory, and reproductive structures. Endoderm forms the respiratory, liver, pancreas, and digestive systems."

EMBRYONIC STAGE

EMBRYONIC STAGE Day 15 → end of week 8

MOST CRITICAL period — organogenesis. All major organs and structures form.

Embryo most susceptible to teratogens (days 17–56 after conception).

Heart begins beating ~22 days. All organ systems present by week 8.

Open speaker notes

"The embryonic stage runs from day fifteen to the end of week eight. This is the most critical period of development. All major organs and external structures form during this time — a process called organogenesis. The embryo is most susceptible to teratogens, specifically days 17 through 56 after conception. The heart begins beating around day 22. By the end of week eight, all organ systems are present in basic form. This is why early exposure to harmful substances is so devastating."

TAKE NOTE: TERATOGENS

TAKE NOTE! TERATOGENIC RISK

Greatest risk: days 17–56 (organogenesis).

Teratogens: → Ionizing radiation, alcohol → Isotretinoin, valproic acid, warfarin → Rubella, CMV, toxoplasmosis, syphilis → Uncontrolled diabetes, lead, mercury

Placenta = pass-through, NOT a barrier.

Open speaker notes

"Take note of this. The period of greatest teratogenic risk is days 17 through 56, during organogenesis. Teratogens include ionizing radiation, alcohol, certain medications such as isotretinoin, valproic acid, and warfarin, infections like rubella, cytomegalovirus, toxoplasmosis, and syphilis, maternal conditions like uncontrolled diabetes, and environmental toxins like lead and mercury. Remember this critical point: the placenta is a pass-through, not a barrier. Almost everything the pregnant person ingests crosses to the fetus."

FETAL STAGE

FETAL STAGE End of week 8 → birth

Longest stage: growth and refinement.

FHR: 110–160 bpm. Quickening felt 16–22 weeks. Viability ~22–24 weeks with care.

Three shunts bypass lungs/liver: → Ductus venosus (liver bypass) → Ductus arteriosus (lung bypass) → Foramen ovale (RA → LA)

Open speaker notes

"The fetal stage runs from the end of week eight until birth. This is the longest stage, characterized by dramatic growth and refinement of all organ systems. The fetal heart rate is 110 to 160 beats per minute. Quickening, the first perception of fetal movement, is felt at 16 to 22 weeks. Viability is approximately 22 to 24 weeks with intensive care. Fetal circulation has three shunts that bypass the lungs and liver: the ductus venosus bypasses the liver, the ductus arteriosus bypasses the lungs, and the foramen ovale shunts blood from the right atrium to the left atrium. These shunts close after birth with the first breath."

SIGNS OF PREGNANCY OVERVIEW

SIGNS OF PREGNANCY

Three categories: → Presumptive (subjective) → Probable (objective, examiner) → Positive (diagnostic, 100%)

Only POSITIVE signs confirm pregnancy with 100% certainty.

Open speaker notes

"Now let us discuss the signs of pregnancy. They are classified into three categories. Presumptive signs are subjective — what the patient perceives. Probable signs are objective — detected by the examiner. Positive signs are diagnostic and confirm pregnancy with one hundred percent certainty. Only positive signs confirm pregnancy definitively. This distinction is tested frequently, so understand it clearly."

PRESUMPTIVE SIGNS

PRESUMPTIVE SIGNS (Subjective)

Perceived by the patient: → Amenorrhea (most obvious) → Nausea/vomiting (~90%) → Breast tenderness → Urinary frequency (no dysuria) → Fatigue → Quickening (~18–20 wk)

NOT diagnostic — many other causes.

Open speaker notes

"Presumptive signs are subjective and perceived by the patient herself. They include amenorrhea, which is the most obvious; nausea and vomiting affecting over ninety percent of patients; breast enlargement and tenderness; urinary frequency without dysuria; excessive fatigue; and quickening, fetal movement felt around 18 to 20 weeks. These signs are not diagnostic. They can be caused by stress, endocrine disorders, gastrointestinal illness, infection, anemia, or oral contraceptives. Do not confirm pregnancy based on presumptive signs alone."

RNSG 1523DAY 0211 / 52

Day 2 of 4

PROBABLE SIGNS

PROBABLE SIGNS (Objective)

Detected by examiner: → Hegar sign (lower segment, ~6 wk) → Goodell sign (cervix soft, ~5 wk) → Chadwick sign (bluish, ~6–8 wk) → Ballottement, Braxton Hicks → Positive pregnancy test (hCG)

Strongly suggestive, NOT 100%.

Open speaker notes

"Probable signs are objective, detected on physical exam by a provider. Hegar sign is softening of the lower uterine segment around six weeks. Goodell sign is softening of the cervix around five weeks. Chadwick sign is a bluish-purple cervix and vagina around six to eight weeks. Ballottement and Braxton Hicks contractions appear between 16 and 28 weeks. A positive pregnancy test measuring hCG is also a probable sign. These are strongly suggestive but not one hundred percent — uterine tumors, pelvic congestion, or molar pregnancy can mimic them."

RNSG 1523DAY 0212 / 52

POSITIVE SIGNS

POSITIVE SIGNS (Diagnostic)

Directly attributable to fetus: → Ultrasound visualization of embryo → Fetal heart tones (Doppler ~10–12 wk) → Fetal movement felt by examiner → FHR on ultrasound (~5–6 wk)

100% confirms pregnancy.

Open speaker notes

"Positive signs are directly attributable to the fetus and only the fetus. They include ultrasound visualization of the embryo or fetus, fetal heart tones heard by Doppler around 10 to 12 weeks or fetoscope around 17 to 20 weeks, fetal movement palpated by an experienced clinician, and fetal heart rate visualized on ultrasound. No other condition produces these findings. Once identified, pregnancy is definitively diagnosed."

hCG INTERPRETATION

hCG — HUMAN CHORIONIC GONADOTROPIN

Made by trophoblast. <5 mIU/mL = negative >25 mIU/mL = positive

Doubles every 29–53 hours. Peaks 8–10 weeks (morning sickness).

Open speaker notes

"Human chorionic gonadotropin, or hCG, is made by the trophoblast. It is the basis of pregnancy tests. A value less than five milli-international units per milliliter is negative. A value greater than 25 is positive. In a viable early pregnancy, hCG doubles every 29 to 53 hours. It peaks at 8 to 10 weeks, which corresponds to the timing of morning sickness. Remember: a positive test is a probable sign, not a positive sign, because other conditions can raise hCG."

GTPAL OVERVIEW

GTPAL — OBSTETRIC HISTORY

GTPAL OVERVIEW quick recall table
LetterMeaning
GGravida — total pregnancies (incl. current)
TTerm births (38–42 wk)
PPreterm births (20–37 wk)
AAbortions (before 20 wk)
LLiving children currently alive
Open speaker notes

"Now let us master GTPAL, the standardized system for documenting obstetric history. G stands for gravida — the total number of pregnancies, including the current one. T stands for term births at 38 to 42 weeks. P stands for preterm births between 20 and 37 weeks. A stands for abortions — pregnancies ending before 20 weeks, whether spontaneous or elective. L stands for living children currently alive. GTPAL provides more clinical detail than the simple gravida and para notation."

GTPAL COUNTING RULES

GTPAL COUNTING RULES

Gravida = every pregnancy + current. Term/Preterm = birth EVENTS, not infants. Abortions = losses before 20 weeks. Living = each child individually.

Multiples = ONE event for T/P/A. Multiples = TWO+ for Living. T + P + A = G − 1

Open speaker notes

"The counting rules are critical. Gravida counts every pregnancy plus the current one. Term and preterm count birth events, not individual infants — twins at term count as one term birth. Abortions count losses before 20 weeks. Living children are counted individually — twins count as two. Multiples count as one event for term, preterm, and abortion, but as individual children for living. The sum of term, preterm, and abortion should equal gravida minus one."

GTPAL EXAMPLE 1

GTPAL WORKED EXAMPLE 1

Mary, pregnant 4th time: → One abortion at 8 weeks → Daughter born at 40 weeks → Son born at 34 weeks

Answer: G4 (1102) G=4, T=1 (40wk), P=1 (34wk), A=1 (8wk), L=2 living children.

Open speaker notes

"Example one. Mary is pregnant for the fourth time. She had one abortion at eight weeks, a daughter born at 40 weeks, and a son born at 34 weeks. Her GTPAL is G4, and in parentheses one-one-zero-two. Gravida is four — three prior pregnancies plus the current one. Term is one — the daughter at 40 weeks. Preterm is one — the son at 34 weeks. Abortions is one — the loss at eight weeks. Living is two — both children are alive."

TAKE NOTE: GTPAL

TAKE NOTE! GTPAL

Always include CURRENT pregnancy in Gravida.

Deceased child: subtract from L, NOT from T, P, or A.

Multiples: one birth event for T/P/A, individual children for L.

GTPAL > gravida/para (more detail).

Open speaker notes

"Take note of these GTPAL fundamentals. Always include the current pregnancy in the gravida count. When a child died after birth — from SIDS, an accident, or neonatal death — subtract them from living only. Do not subtract from term, preterm, or abortion, because those reflect the birth outcome, not survival. Multiples count once for parity but individually for living children. And GTPAL is preferred over gravida and para alone because it provides more clinical detail."

NAGELE'S RULE

EDD — NAGELE'S RULE

Step 1: First day of LMP. Step 2: Subtract 3 months. Step 3: Add 7 days. Step 4: Add 1 year.

Margin of error: ±2 weeks.

Open speaker notes

"Now the estimated date of delivery, or EDD, also called the estimated date of birth. Naegele's rule calculates it from the first day of the last menstrual period. Step one, identify the first day of the LMP. Step two, subtract three months. Step three, add seven days. Step four, add one year. The margin of error is plus or minus two weeks. Birth two weeks before or after the EDD is considered normal."

TAKE NOTE: OVULATION RULE

TAKE NOTE! OVULATION TIMING

Ovulation = 14 days before NEXT menses, regardless of cycle length.

35-day cycle → ovulation day 21. 21-day cycle → ovulation day 7.

Naegele's rule assumes 28-day cycle. Irregular cycles → use ULTRASOUND.

Open speaker notes

"Take note of the ovulation timing rule. Ovulation always occurs fourteen days before the next menses begins, regardless of cycle length. For a 35-day cycle, ovulation occurs on day 21. For a 21-day cycle, ovulation occurs on day seven. This is why Naegele's rule is less accurate with irregular cycles — it assumes a 28-day cycle with ovulation on day fourteen. When cycles are irregular, ultrasound dating supersedes Naegele's rule. Always verify the EDD with ultrasound when available."

HEALTH HISTORY COMPONENTS

→ Reason for care: LMP, signs, hCG. → Past medical/surgical, allergies. → Reproductive: menstrual, GTPAL, GYN. → Family/partner: genetics, blood type. → Screen for intimate partner violence (IPV).

Open speaker notes

PHYSICAL EXAM COMPONENTS

→ Vitals, height, weight, BMI. → Head-to-toe assessment. → Pelvic exam: Goodell, Hegar, Chadwick. → Pap smear, STI cultures. → Pelvic measurements (primigravida).

LABORATORY TESTS

LABORATORY TESTS

→ CBC: Hgb 12–14, Hct 42%, platelets. → Blood type & Rh factor. → Rubella titer, Hep B, HIV, syphilis. → Cervical cultures, GBS at 35–37 wk. → Urinalysis, Pap, ultrasound.

Open speaker notes

"Laboratory tests include a complete blood count with hemoglobin of 12 to 14, hematocrit around 42 percent, and platelets. Cervical cultures screen for gonorrhea and chlamydia, and Group B Streptococcus is cultured at 35 to 37 weeks. Urinalysis, Pap smear, and ultrasound complete the initial workup."

MATERNAL ADAPTATIONS: REPRODUCTIVE

REPRODUCTIVE ADAPTATIONS

Uterus: size ×20, 70g → 1,100g. Fundus: 12wk symphysis, 20wk umbilicus, 36wk xiphoid. Lightening 38–40 wk.

Cervix: Goodell, mucus plug, Chadwick. Vagina: vascularity, leukorrhea. Breasts: tenderness, colostrum.

Open speaker notes

"Pregnancy changes virtually every body system. In the reproductive system, the uterus increases in size by twenty times, growing from 70 grams to 1,100 grams. Fundal height landmarks are testable: 12 weeks the fundus is at the symphysis pubis, 16 weeks midway to the umbilicus, 20 weeks at the umbilicus, 36 weeks at the xiphoid process, and lightening occurs at 38 to 40 weeks. The cervix shows Goodell sign, forms a mucus plug, and shows Chadwick sign. The vagina has increased vascularity and leukorrhea. Breasts enlarge, become tender, and produce colostrum by the third trimester."

MATERNAL ADAPTATIONS: CARDIOVASCULAR

CARDIOVASCULAR ADAPTATIONS

Blood volume ↑ 30–50%, peaks 30–34 wk. Plasma ↑ > RBC → physiologic anemia.

Cardiac output ↑ 30–40% (peaks 20–24 wk). HR ↑ 10–15 bpm. BP ↓ mid-pregnancy (progesterone). Hypercoagulable → ↑ DVT risk.

Open speaker notes

"Cardiovascular adaptations are profound. Blood volume increases thirty to fifty percent, peaking at 30 to 34 weeks. Plasma increases more than red blood cells, causing physiologic anemia — this is a normal dilutional effect, not a true deficiency. Cardiac output increases thirty to forty percent, peaking at 20 to 24 weeks. Heart rate rises 10 to 15 beats per minute. Blood pressure drops slightly in mid-pregnancy due to progesterone vasodilation. Clotting factors increase and venous stasis develops, creating a hypercoagulable state with increased DVT risk. Any increase in blood pressure is abnormal and must be investigated for gestational hypertension."

MATERNAL ADAPTATIONS: RESPIRATORY & GI

RESPIRATORY & GI ADAPTATIONS

Respiratory: → Tidal volume ↑ 30–40% → Diaphragm rises 4 cm → Nasal stuffiness, epistaxis

GI: → ↓ LES tone → pyrosis → ↓ Peristalsis → constipation → N/V ≥90% (hCG peak)

Open speaker notes

"Respiratory adaptations include increased tidal volume of thirty to forty percent and increased oxygen consumption. The diaphragm rises about four centimeters, and thoracic breathing replaces abdominal breathing. Nasal stuffiness and epistaxis occur from estrogen. In the gastrointestinal system, progesterone relaxes the lower esophageal sphincter, causing heartburn. Peristalsis slows, causing constipation. Nausea and vomiting affect over ninety percent of patients, begin around six weeks, and resolve by 20 weeks."

MATERNAL ADAPTATIONS: RENAL & OTHER

RENAL & OTHER ADAPTATIONS

Renal: ↑ GFR, ureter dilation, ↑ UTI risk, urinary frequency.

Musculoskeletal: lordosis, joint laxity, round ligament pain.

Integumentary: linea nigra, melasma, striae gravidarum.

Endocrine: hCG, hCS, prolactin, insulin resistance (GDM risk).

Open speaker notes

"Renal adaptations include increased glomerular filtration rate, dilation of the renal pelvis and ureters, and increased UTI risk. Musculoskeletal changes include lordosis, joint laxity from relaxin and progesterone, and round ligament pain. Integumentary changes include linea nigra, melasma, and striae gravidarum. Endocrine changes include placental hormone production — hCG, hCS, and prolactin — along with increased insulin resistance, which can unmask gestational diabetes. Screen for GDM at 24 to 28 weeks."

TAKE NOTE: SUPINE HYPOTENSION

TAKE NOTE! SUPINE HYPOTENSION

Gravid uterus compresses IVC when supine (after 20 weeks).

Symptoms: dizziness, nausea, tachycardia, hypotension, syncope.

Action: position LEFT LATERAL or wedge under right hip.

Mechanical complication — NOT a disease.

Open speaker notes

"Take note of supine hypotensive syndrome. After 20 weeks, the gravid uterus compresses the inferior vena cava when the patient lies supine. This reduces venous return, cardiac output, and blood pressure. Symptoms are dizziness, lightheadedness, nausea, tachycardia, hypotension, and syncope. The nursing action is to position the patient in the left lateral position, or place a wedge under the right hip if supine is required. Remember, this is a mechanical complication, not a disease."

FOLLOW-UP VISIT SCHEDULE

FOLLOW-UP VISIT SCHEDULE

Every 4 weeks → conception to 28 weeks. Every 2 weeks → 29 to 36 weeks. Every week → 37 weeks to birth.

Assess: weight, BP, urine, fundal height, FHR, fetal movement, Leopold maneuvers.

Open speaker notes

"The follow-up visit schedule is straightforward. Every four weeks from conception to 28 weeks. Every two weeks from 29 to 36 weeks. Every week from 37 weeks until birth. At every visit, assess weight and blood pressure, urine dipstick for protein and glucose, fundal height, fetal heart rate, fetal movement, and perform Leopold maneuvers after 36 weeks. Trend these measurements across visits — a rising blood pressure trend warrants evaluation even if a single reading looks normal."

DANGER SIGNS

DANGER SIGNS — REPORT IMMEDIATELY

1st tri: bleeding, severe vomiting, fever, ectopic symptoms.

2nd tri: contractions, fluid leak, absent movement >12h, calf pain.

3rd tri: edema, epigastric pain, headache + vision, bleeding.

Open speaker notes

"Danger signs require immediate reporting. In the first trimester: bleeding, severe persistent vomiting, fever over 100 degrees, painful urination, and lower abdominal pain with dizziness and shoulder pain — which suggests a ruptured ectopic pregnancy. In the second trimester: regular contractions suggesting preterm labor, sudden gush of fluid suggesting PROM, absent fetal movement over 12 hours, and calf pain with dorsiflexion suggesting DVT. In the third trimester: sudden weight gain, facial and hand edema, severe epigastric pain, headache with visual changes suggesting preeclampsia, and late bleeding suggesting previa or abruption."

TAKE NOTE: FETAL MOVEMENT

TAKE NOTE! FETAL MOVEMENT

Quickening: first felt 16–22 weeks. Earlier in multiparas.

"Count to 10" method: Lie left side, count movements daily.

If >2 hours to reach 10 movements → contact provider immediately.

Decreased movement = possible hypoxia.

Open speaker notes

"Take note of fetal movement assessment. Quickening is the first perception of fetal movement, felt at 16 to 22 weeks, earlier in multiparous patients. The count-to-ten method: the patient lies on her left side and counts fetal movements at the same time daily, recording how long it takes to feel ten movements. If it takes more than two hours to reach ten movements, she must contact her provider immediately. Decreased fetal movement is a sign of possible fetal hypoxia — the fetus conserves oxygen by reducing activity."

KEY TERMS: SIGNS OF PREGNANCY

KEY TERMINOLOGY — SIGNS OF PREGNANCY

"Quick recall. Preembryonic stage: zygote to morula to blastocyst. Embryonic stage: organogenesis — the most critical period, when all major organs form and teratogenic risk is highest. Fetal stage: growth and refinement, with viability around 22 to 24 weeks. The three fetal shunts are the ductus venosus, ductus arteriosus, and foramen ovale. Presumptive signs are subjective, probable signs are examiner-observed, and positive signs are fetal heartbeat, movement, or ultrasound visualization."
