## 02 MENSTRUAL CYCLE REVIEW

The menstrual cycle is regulated by the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian axis. Normal cycles range from 21 to 35 days, with menstruation lasting ≤8 days. Average blood loss is ≤80 mL per cycle.

### Hormone Review

| Hormone | Source | Primary Function |
| --- | --- | --- |
| GnRH | Hypothalamus | Stimulates pituitary to release FSH and LH |
| FSH | Anterior pituitary | Stimulates follicle maturation; highest in follicular phase |
| LH | Anterior pituitary | Triggers ovulation (LH surge); supports corpus luteum |
| Estrogen | Ovarian follicle | Proliferates endometrium; cervical mucus thin & stretchy |
| Progesterone | Corpus luteum | Secretory endometrium; maintains pregnancy; raises BBT |
| Prostaglandins | Endometrium | Uterine contractions; key role in dysmenorrhea pain |

### Ovarian Cycle (hormonal events)

- **Follicular phase (Day 1–14):** FSH stimulates follicle growth; estrogen rises; endometrium proliferates.
- **Ovulation (Day 14 in 28-day cycle):** LH surge → follicle ruptures → mature oocyte released. Ovulation occurs 14 days before next menses, regardless of cycle length.
- **Luteal phase (Day 15–28):** Corpus luteum secretes progesterone; endometrium becomes secretory. If no fertilization, corpus luteum degenerates → hormone drop → menstruation.

### Endometrial (Uterine) Cycle

- **Proliferative phase:** Estrogen-driven; endometrium thickens; coincides with follicular phase.
- **Secretory phase:** Progesterone-driven; glands secrete glycogen; coincides with luteal phase.
- **Ischemic phase:** Arteriole spasm → tissue ischemia if no implantation.
- **Menstrual phase:** Spiral arteries rupture → endometrial shedding → menses.

### Key Cycle Concepts

- **Cervical mucus at ovulation:** thin, clear, stretchy (spinnbarkeit); otherwise thick and impenetrable.
- **Basal body temperature (BBT):** rises 0.5°F–1°F after ovulation due to progesterone; remains elevated in pregnancy.
- **Mittelschmerz:** mid-cycle unilateral pain at ovulation (~40% of those who ovulate).
- **Menarche:** average age 12.4 years (range 10–16); influenced by genetics, nutrition, and health.

## 03 DYSMENORRHEA (PAINFUL MENSTRUATION)

Dysmenorrhea affects 50%–90% of menstruating persons and is the leading cause of school/work absenteeism related to gynecologic health. Risk factors: younger age, smoking, stress.

| Feature | PRIMARY (Spasmodic) | SECONDARY (Congestive) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cause | Increased prostaglandin production by the endometrium in an ovulatory cycle | Underlying pelvic pathology (endometriosis, fibroids, PID, adenomyosis, IUD) |
| Onset | Within a few years of menarche; begins shortly after ovulatory cycles establish | Years after relatively painless periods; typically third or fourth decade of life |
| Pain Timing | 1–2 days before bleeding starts; peaks as flow becomes heaviest (days 1–2) | May begin before menses; pain worsens progressively over months to years |
| Associated Sx | Suprapubic cramping; N/V; diarrhea; headache; fatigue; general malaise | Dyspareunia; heavy or irregular bleeding; infertility; chronic pelvic pain |
| Pelvic Exam | Normal | May reveal masses, tenderness, nodularity, or fixed retroverted uterus |
| Common Causes | Prostaglandin-mediated vasoconstriction and increased uterine contractions | Endometriosis (most common cause); adenomyosis; fibroids; PID; cervical stenosis |
| First-Line Tx | NSAIDs (ibuprofen 400–800 mg q6–8h or naproxen 250–550 mg q6–8h) + hormonal contraceptives (combined OCs, ring, patch) | Treat underlying cause; NSAIDs + hormonal contraceptives; if no improvement in 3–6 months → diagnostic laparoscopy |
| Other Tx | Heat therapy; exercise; yoga; acupuncture; acupressure; massage; dietary changes | Laparoscopic excision of endometriosis; GnRH agonists; endometrial ablation; hysterectomy |

> **■ TAKE NOTE!** Start NSAIDs **BEFORE** pain becomes severe. NSAIDs work by inhibiting prostaglandin synthesis. For maximum effect, begin 1–2 days before expected menses onset and continue on a scheduled (not PRN) basis through days 1–2 of flow. Ibuprofen 400–800 mg q6–8h (max 2,400 mg/day) or naproxen 250–550 mg q6–8h. Take with food. If no relief in 3–6 months → further investigation for secondary causes. Exercise increases endorphins and suppresses prostaglandin release. Limit caffeine, alcohol, and smoking (all cause vasoconstriction).

**Discrimination pearl:** Primary dysmenorrhea = painful menses **without pelvic pathology** (prostaglandin-mediated, responds to NSAIDs). Secondary dysmenorrhea = painful menses **caused by identifiable disease** (endometriosis, fibroids).

## 04 MENOPAUSAL TRANSITION & MENOPAUSE

Menopause: permanent cessation of menses — defined as **1 year without a menstrual period**. Average age: 51.4 years (range 47–55). Perimenopause: transition period from onset of irregular cycles to the final menstrual period; begins ~age 47; can last 7–14 years. Menopause is a natural maturing process, not a disease. More than 1.3 million people in the U.S. experience menopause annually.

### Systemic Effects of Declining Estrogen

| Body System | Changes Due to Estrogen Decline |
| --- | --- |
| CNS / Brain | Hot flashes & night sweats (vasomotor instability — ~80% affected; caused by inappropriate peripheral vasodilation); sleep disturbance; mood changes, irritability, depression, anxiety; "brain fog" and forgetfulness; cognitive changes |
| Cardiovascular | ↓ HDL; ↑ CVD risk (#1 cause of death in females); atypical MI signs in females — use ABCDEFGH: Angina, Breathlessness, Chronic fatigue, Dizziness, Edema of hands/feet, Fluttering, Gastric upset, Heavy back/shoulder pain. Estrogen is cardioprotective pre-menopause. |
| Skeletal | Rapid bone density loss → osteoporosis risk; joint aches and pains; 1 in 2 females >50 will have an osteoporosis-related fracture; decreased calcium absorption from food |
| Genitourinary | Genitourinary Syndrome of Menopause (GSM): vaginal dryness, irritation, itching; dyspareunia; ↑ vaginal pH → ↑ infection risk; urinary urgency, frequency, stress incontinence; labial/vaginal atrophy. Progressive — does NOT resolve without treatment. |
| Integumentary | Dry, thin skin; ↓ collagen → wrinkling; hair changes |
| Body Composition | ↑ abdominal fat; ↓ lean muscle mass; waist-to-hip ratio increases |
| Breasts | Tenderness; decreased tissue density; replacement of glandular tissue with adipose tissue |

### Management of Menopausal Symptoms

- **Vasomotor symptoms (hot flashes/night sweats):** MHT is gold standard for moderate-to-severe symptoms. **Non-hormonal:** SSRIs/SNRIs (venlafaxine, paroxetine, escitalopram), gabapentin, oxybutynin, fezolinetant (NK3R antagonist, FDA-approved 2023). Lifestyle: layered clothing, cool environment, avoid triggers (caffeine, alcohol, spicy foods, stress, hot environments).
- **Genitourinary Syndrome (GSM):** Vaginal moisturizers + lubricants (OTC); low-dose vaginal estrogen (cream, tablet, ring — Estring); ospemifene (oral SERM). GSM is chronic and progressive; symptoms worsen over time if untreated. Assess sensitively — many patients do not report due to embarrassment.
- **Osteoporosis prevention:** Calcium 1,200 mg/day + Vitamin D 800 IU/day; weight-bearing exercise 30+ min/day; DEXA screening at baseline menopause; bisphosphonates (alendronate, risedronate); SERMs (raloxifene); denosumab; romosozumab.

> **■ TAKE NOTE!** CVD is the LEADING cause of death in females. Female patients often present with atypical cardiac symptoms compared to males: **A**ngina (chest pain); **B**reathlessness; **C**hronic fatigue; **D**izziness; **E**dema of hands/feet; **F**luttering; **G**astric upset; **H**eavy back/shoulder pain. Nurses: do NOT dismiss these symptoms — CVD is still often misperceived as a "man's disease," leading to delayed diagnosis and suboptimal treatment in female patients.

**Discrimination pearls:**
- **VMS vs GSM** — Vasomotor symptoms (VMS) are hot flashes/night sweats; GSM is vaginal + urinary tissue change. Treated differently.
- **MHT risk window** — Menopausal hormone therapy is safest when started under age 60 or within 10 years of menopause.
- **Postmenopausal bleeding** — Any bleeding after menopause requires endometrial evaluation to exclude cancer (cancer until proven otherwise).
- **Abnormal uterine bleeding** — Always evaluate postmenopausal bleeding for cancer.

## 05 CONTRACEPTIVE METHODS

90% of U.S. females of reproductive age who do not want pregnancy use some form of contraception. The ideal method is safe, effective, easy to use, reversible, and culturally acceptable. Nurses play a key role in informed, shared decision-making — the best method is the one used consistently.

| Category / Method | Mechanism | Failure* | Key Points |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **BEHAVIORAL METHODS** |  |  |  |
| Fertility awareness (BBT, Billings, SDM, symptothermal) | Avoid intercourse during fertile window; track BBT, cervical mucus, cycle days | 23% | No side effects; acceptable to most religions. Requires daily tracking, discipline, and regular cycles. High failure with incorrect use. |
| Withdrawal (coitus interruptus) | Penis withdrawn from vagina before ejaculation | 22% | No devices or cost; always available. Requires significant male self-control. Pre-ejaculate may contain sperm. No STI protection. |
| Lactational amenorrhea method (LAM) | Exclusive breastfeeding suppresses ovulation via prolactin | 0.45–7.5% | Only effective first 6 months postpartum IF exclusively breastfeeding (no supplements) AND amenorrheic. No cost; not coitus-linked. |
| **BARRIER METHODS** |  |  |  |
| Male condom | Thin sheath over erect penis blocks sperm entry into vagina | 13% | ONLY method that protects against STIs (including HIV). Widely available OTC; low cost. Check for latex allergy → polyurethane alternatives. Single use. |
| Female condom | Polyurethane sheath inserted vaginally; lines vagina and external genitalia | 21% | Female-controlled; provides STI protection. Expensive for frequent use; cumbersome; may be noisy during intercourse. Single use only. |
| Diaphragm + spermicide | Shallow latex cup with spring rim covers cervix; spermicide kills sperm | 17% | Must be professionally fitted. Refit after 15-lb weight change, full-term pregnancy, or pelvic surgery. Leave in place 6 h after intercourse. ↑ UTI risk. |
| Cervical cap + spermicide | Soft cup-shaped latex device fits snugly over base of cervix | 17–23% | Must be fitted. Can stay in place up to 48 h. Higher failure rate in parous persons. Odor may occur if left in place too long. |
| Contraceptive sponge | Disk-shaped polyurethane device with spermicide; activated by wetting with water | 14% nullip / 27% parous | OTC; 24-hour continuous protection. Can fall out with voiding. Remove within 30 h after insertion. Risk of toxic shock syndrome. |
| **HORMONAL METHODS** |  |  |  |
| Combined OCs (estrogen + progestin) | Suppress ovulation; thicken cervical mucus; thin endometrium | 7% | Contraindications: smoking >35 yo, HTN, migraines with aura, Hx DVT/PE, breast cancer, liver disease, thrombophlebitis. Benefits: regulate cycles, ↓ ovarian & endometrial cancer risk, ↓ acne. Teach ACHES danger signs. |
| Progestin-only pills (mini-pill) | Thickens cervical mucus to block sperm; may suppress ovulation | 7% | Safe for lactating patients and those with estrogen contraindications. MUST take at same time daily (±3 hours). May cause irregular bleeding. Slightly higher ectopic pregnancy risk if failure occurs. |
| Transdermal patch (Ortho Evra) | Releases estrogen + progestin through skin into circulation | 7% | Weekly patch change × 3 weeks, then 1 week off. May cause skin irritation. Less effective if patient weighs >200 lb. May fall off unnoticed. |
| Vaginal ring (NuvaRing) | Flexible 2-inch ring inserted vaginally; releases estrogen + progestin | 7% | Insert for 3 weeks, remove for 1 week. Monthly regimen. May cause vaginal discharge or irritation. Does not require daily adherence. |
| Injectable (Depo-Provera) | Progestin injection (DMPA) IM q12 weeks; suppresses ovulation | 4% | ↓ Bone mineral density with long-term use (≥2 years) — reversible after discontinuation. Return to fertility may be delayed up to 18 months. Weight gain common. Irregular bleeding initially; amenorrhea over time. |
| Implant (Nexplanon) | Single progestin rod inserted subdermally in upper arm; lasts 3 years | 0.05% | Most effective reversible contraceptive available. Quick return to fertility after removal. Irregular bleeding is most common side effect and reason for discontinuation. Requires trained provider for insertion/removal. |
| IUD-LNG (Mirena, Kyleena, Skyla) | T-shaped device releases levonorgestrel; thickens cervical mucus; thins endometrium | 0.2% | Lasts 3–8 years depending on brand. ↓ Menstrual bleeding over time; may cause amenorrhea. Progestin-only — safe for estrogen contraindications. Check string placement regularly. |
| IUD-Copper (ParaGard) | Copper ions toxic to sperm; creates sterile inflammatory reaction | 0.8% | Non-hormonal — ideal for patients who cannot or prefer not to use hormones. Lasts up to 10 years. May increase menstrual bleeding and cramping. Also FDA-approved as emergency contraception (>99% effective up to 5 days). |
| **PERMANENT METHODS** |  |  |  |
| Tubal ligation | Fallopian tubes are blocked, cut, or sealed (laparoscopic or postpartum) | 0.5% | Permanent; considered irreversible. Can be performed immediately postpartum (within 24–48 h of vaginal delivery) or as interval procedure. Requires surgical consent; discuss regret risk with patient. |
| Vasectomy | Vas deferens is cut, tied, or blocked to prevent sperm in ejaculate | 0.15% | Less invasive than tubal ligation; office procedure under local anesthesia. Not immediately effective — must use backup contraception until semen analysis confirms azoospermia (usually after 12 weeks / 20 ejaculations). |

\* = typical use (% of users who become pregnant in first year). Perfect-use rates are lower. Source: Trussell (2018), CDC, ACOG, Ricci et al. (2020).

### Emergency Contraception (EC)

- **Levonorgestrel (Plan B One-Step):** OTC, no age restriction. Most effective within 72 h (up to 120 h). Delays ovulation. Does NOT terminate an existing pregnancy.
- **Ulipristal acetate (ella):** Rx only. Effective up to 120 h (5 days). More effective than LNG, especially days 3–5. May interact with progestin-containing contraceptives.
- **Copper IUD (ParaGard):** Most effective EC (>99%). Can be inserted up to 5 days after unprotected intercourse. Provides ongoing long-term contraception. Requires provider visit.

> **■ TAKE NOTE!** Contraceptive counseling is a nursing priority. Use shared decision-making. Ask: "What are your reproductive goals in the next year?" Assess: medical history, contraindications, lifestyle, cultural/religious preferences, STI risk, desire for future fertility, and ability to adhere. Teach **ACHES** for estrogen-containing methods: **A**bdominal pain (severe), **C**hest pain/SOB, **H**eadache (severe), **E**ye problems (vision changes), **S**evere leg pain (DVT). If any ACHES sign → stop method and seek immediate evaluation.

### High-Yield Contraception Pearls

- **Estrogen contraindications (COC):** migraine **with aura**, VTE history, smoker ≥35. Migraine **without** aura is generally less restrictive — the aura status, not merely "migraine," drives estrogen eligibility.
- **POP vs COC eligibility:** Estrogen (COC) is contraindicated with high VTE or migraine-with-aura risk; progestin-only (POP) is safer there.
- **LARC vs short-acting:** LARC (IUD, implant) needs no daily adherence and is the most effective reversible option; pills/rings depend on consistent use.
- **Typical-use effectiveness:** the real-world failure rate — the number to counsel patients on.
- **IUD danger signs (PATCH mnemonic):** **P**eriod late, **A**bdominal pain, **C**hills/fever, **T**rouble or foul discharge, **H**eavy bleeding.

> **The three things most likely to be tested:** (1) migraine **with aura** contraindicates estrogen; (2) the **copper IUD** is the most effective emergency contraception; (3) LARC = highest effectiveness with no daily adherence.

## 06 PRECONCEPTION CARE & GENETICS

**Preconception care = promoting health before pregnancy to identify and modify biomedical, behavioral, and social risks.** Goal: optimize maternal health for the best pregnancy outcomes. Should occur at any health care encounter with a person of childbearing potential. The period of greatest environmental sensitivity for the embryo is days 17–56 after conception (organogenesis) — often before the person knows they are pregnant.

### Key Preconception Interventions

- **Folic acid 400–800 mcg/day** — reduces neural tube defects by two-thirds. Higher dose (4 mg/day) if prior NTD-affected pregnancy, on antiepileptics, or with diabetes. Begin **before** conception — the neural tube closes very early.
- **Immunizations:** Influenza annually; Tdap (each pregnancy); rubella & varicella if not immune (live vaccines → avoid pregnancy for 1 month); hepatitis B; HPV.
- **Achieve healthy BMI (18.5–24.9 kg/m²)** before pregnancy. Obesity (BMI >30) increases risk of preeclampsia, GDM, cesarean birth, and stillbirth.
- **Manage chronic conditions:** Diabetes (optimize HbA1c), hypertension, thyroid disorders, asthma, seizure disorders — transition to pregnancy-safe medications before conception.
- **Review ALL medications:** Discontinue known teratogens; change to safer alternatives. Common teratogens: isotretinoin, valproic acid, warfarin, ACE inhibitors, tetracycline.
- **Screen for STIs:** Chlamydia, gonorrhea, syphilis, HIV. Early treatment prevents ectopic pregnancy, infertility, PID, and neonatal transmission.

### Risk Factors for Adverse Pregnancy Outcomes

| Risk Factor | Potential Adverse Outcome |
| --- | --- |
| Isotretinoins (Accutane) | Cleft palate, congenital heart defects, hearing loss, microcephaly, hydrocephaly, intellectual disability |
| Alcohol | Fetal alcohol spectrum disorder (FASD); neurodevelopmental deficits; growth restriction; characteristic facial features. No safe amount or trimester. |
| Antiepileptic drugs (valproic acid) | Neural tube defects (spina bifida); cognitive deficits; structural malformations; developmental delay. Transition to safer alternatives preconception. |
| Diabetes (uncontrolled) | ↑ Birth defects (cardiac, neural tube, renal); macrosomia → shoulder dystocia; stillbirth; neonatal hypoglycemia. Optimize HbA1c <6.5% preconception. |
| Folic acid deficiency | Neural tube defects: spina bifida, anencephaly, encephalocele. Supplementation reduces risk by up to 70%. |
| Obesity (BMI >30) | Preeclampsia; GDM; cesarean birth; stillbirth; macrosomia; congenital anomalies; increased NICU admission |
| Smoking | Preterm birth; low birth weight; stillbirth; SIDS; placental abruption; placenta previa; orofacial clefts |
| Hypertension | Preeclampsia/eclampsia; placental abruption; IUGR; preterm birth; maternal stroke; renal failure |
| STIs (chlamydia, gonorrhea, syphilis) | Ectopic pregnancy; infertility; chronic pelvic pain/PID; neonatal conjunctivitis and pneumonia; congenital syphilis |

### Patterns of Inheritance — Quick Reference

| Pattern | Key Feature | Example Disorders | Recurrence Risk |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Autosomal Dominant | One mutant gene → phenotype expressed. Affected person usually has one affected parent. 50% chance each offspring inherits. Males and females equally affected. Male-to-male transmission possible. Variable expression and reduced penetrance may occur. | Huntington disease; Marfan syndrome; neurofibromatosis; achondroplasia; osteogenesis imperfecta type 1 | 50% |
| Autosomal Recessive | Two copies of mutant gene needed (homozygous) to express phenotype. Both parents are obligate carriers (heterozygous, clinically unaffected). Males and females equally affected. Typically seen in only one generation. Consanguinity increases risk. | Cystic fibrosis; sickle cell disease; Tay-Sachs disease; PKU; galactosemia; thalassemias | 25% affected / 50% carrier / 25% unaffected |
| X-linked Recessive | Males predominantly affected (only one X chromosome). No male-to-male transmission. Carrier female → 50% of sons affected, 50% of daughters carriers. Affected male → all daughters carriers, no sons affected. | Hemophilia A/B; Duchenne muscular dystrophy; color blindness; G6PD deficiency | 50% sons affected / 50% daughters carriers |
| X-linked Dominant | Affected male → all daughters affected, no sons affected. Affected female → 50% of offspring affected (both sexes). Males more severely affected. Many X-linked dominant disorders are lethal in males. | Hypophosphatemic (vitamin D-resistant) rickets; Rett syndrome (lethal in males); incontinentia pigmenti | 50% (if one parent affected) |
| Multifactorial | Multiple genes + environmental factors interact. Runs in families but pattern less predictable. Risk increases with closer degree of relationship. Often a sex bias present. Not 100% concordant in identical twins → confirms environmental role. | Cleft lip/palate; spina bifida; pyloric stenosis; congenital hip dysplasia; clubfoot; many congenital heart defects | 2–5% (varies by condition and family history) |

> **■ TAKE NOTE!** Ideal time for genetic counseling is **BEFORE conception**. Preconception carrier screening allows couples to understand their risks, explore reproductive options, and plan for early prenatal diagnosis if desired. The Genetic Information Nondiscrimination Act (GINA, 2008) protects against discrimination by health insurers and employers, but does NOT extend to life, disability, or long-term care insurance. Nurses provide nondirective, nonjudgmental information and support — the ultimate reproductive decision rests solely with the patient/couple.

## 08 NGN UNFOLDING CASE — Adolescent Contraception Counseling

**Jordan, 16**, presents to a school-based clinic requesting contraception. Reports occasional migraine **with aura**. No chronic conditions, nonsmoker. Wants a method she does not have to take daily.

*(Note: verify Texas minors' consent/confidentiality rules locally — never assume.)*

**Key learning points through the case:**

1. **Recognize Cues:** Age 16; migraine **with aura**; nonsmoker; sexually active; preference for a non-daily method. *Migraine with aura is the key estrogen-eligibility cue; the non-daily preference points toward LARC.*
2. **Analyze Cues (method eligibility):** Combined oral contraceptive → **estrogen concern — avoid combined**. Levonorgestrel IUD, etonogestrel implant, progestin-only pill → **generally appropriate**. *Migraine with aura makes estrogen-containing methods a higher stroke risk; progestin-only/LARC/non-hormonal remain appropriate.*
3. **Prioritize:** Prioritize discussing **LARC**; avoid **combined estrogen-progestin methods**.
4. **Generate Solutions:** Present LARC and progestin-only options with typical-use effectiveness; provide condom teaching for STI prevention regardless of method; explain confidentiality/consent within local rules.
5. **Take Actions:** Teach ACHES/PATCH-relevant warning signs for the chosen method; document education and shared decision; offer STI screening and condoms.
6. **Evaluate:** Continuation, tolerability, satisfaction, and STI protection over time — not a single visit.

**Debrief — three decision points students most often miss:**
- **Overlooking aura** — "Migraine" alone is not the discriminator; **aura** is what restricts estrogen.
- **Forgetting dual protection** — any hormonal or LARC method still needs condom teaching for STIs.
- **Assuming consent rules** — minors' consent and confidentiality are set by state law and must be verified locally.

## 10 ANSWER KEY & RATIONALE

| Q | Ans | Rationale |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | B | Primary dysmenorrhea = prostaglandin-mediated pain with normal pelvic exam. NSAIDs started 1–2 days pre-menses and taken on a scheduled basis most effectively suppress prostaglandin synthesis. Laparoscopy (A) is for suspected secondary causes; hysterectomy (C) is definitive but not first-line for a 19-year-old; antibiotics (D) are for PID, which would present with abnormal exam findings. |
| 2 | C | Menopause management is individualized. GSM does NOT resolve without treatment (eliminating A). MHT is effective but not the only option — non-hormonal therapies (SSRIs, gabapentin, fezolinetant) and lifestyle changes also work (eliminating B). Black cohosh (D) lacks strong evidence of efficacy. |
| 3 | C | Combined OCs contain estrogen, which increases thromboembolic risk and is contraindicated in patients with a history of DVT/PE. Progestin-only methods and the non-hormonal copper IUD are safe alternatives. |
| 4 | B | Valproic acid is a known teratogen (neural tube defects, cognitive deficits). Priority: collaborate with neurology to find a safer anticonvulsant and initiate high-dose folic acid (4 mg/day). Never abruptly discontinue antiepileptics (D). The 400 mcg dose (C) is insufficient for patients on antiepileptics. (A) is directive, not therapeutic. |
| 5 | B | Migraine without aura is less restrictive than migraine with aura, where estrogen is avoided due to stroke risk. The aura status, not merely "migraine," drives estrogen eligibility. |
| 6 | C | The copper IUD is the most effective emergency contraception and provides ongoing contraception; it uniquely doubles as emergency and ongoing contraception. |
| 7 | A | PATCH captures IUD danger signs pointing to infection, expulsion, or perforation. |
| 8 | B | In a regular 28-day cycle, ovulation occurs around day 14, midway between menses and the next cycle. |

## 11 QUICK RECALL

| TOPIC | ONE-LINE MEMORY HOOK |
| --- | --- |
| Menstrual cycle sequence | FSH grows follicle → LH surge → ovulation → corpus luteum makes progesterone |
| Primary dysmenorrhea | Prostaglandin pain with normal exam; treat with scheduled NSAIDs 1–2 days pre-menses |
| Secondary dysmenorrhea | Pain from pelvic pathology (endometriosis #1); worsens over time; abnormal exam; treat underlying cause |
| Menopause definition | 1 full year without menses; average age 51; perimenopause = transitional years before final period |
| Hot flashes / night sweats | Vasomotor instability from ↓ estrogen; ~80% affected; MHT = gold standard for moderate-severe |
| Genitourinary syndrome (GSM) | Vaginal dryness + urinary sx from estrogen loss; progressive — does NOT self-resolve |
| Osteoporosis prevention | Calcium 1,200 mg + Vit D 800 IU + weight-bearing exercise daily; DEXA screening at menopause baseline |
| CVD in females | #1 killer of females; atypical sx vs males → ABCDEFGH; do not dismiss fatigue/SOB/back pain |
| Combined OCs — contraindications | Smoking >35, HTN, migraine with aura, Hx DVT/PE, breast Ca, liver disease, thrombophlebitis |
| ACHES danger signs (OCs) | Abdominal pain (severe); Chest pain/SOB; Headache (severe); Eye problems; Severe leg pain |
| PATCH danger signs (IUD) | Period late; Abdominal pain; Chills/fever; Trouble/foul discharge; Heavy bleeding |
| Most effective reversible method | Implant (Nexplanon) 0.05% failure; followed by IUDs (LNG 0.2%, Cu 0.8%) |
| Only STI-protective methods | Male condom (13% failure) and female condom (21% failure) — all other methods = no STI protection |
| Best emergency contraception | Copper IUD (>99%, up to 5 days) > ulipristal > levonorgestrel |
| Folic acid dosing | 400 mcg/day routine; 4 mg/day if prior NTD, on antiepileptics, or pregestational diabetes |
| Preconception key question | "Would you like to become pregnant in the next year?" → guides contraception vs optimization counseling |
| Teratogenic window | Days 17–56 after conception = organogenesis — the period of greatest environmental sensitivity |
| Genetic counseling ideal timing | Before conception — allows carrier screening, informed reproductive planning, early prenatal diagnosis |
