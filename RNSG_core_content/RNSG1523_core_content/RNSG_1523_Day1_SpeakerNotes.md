## SLIDE 04 — MENSTRUAL CYCLE OVERVIEW
"Let us begin with the menstrual cycle. The cycle is regulated by the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian axis. Normal cycles range from 21 to 35 days. Menstruation itself should last no more than eight days, and the average blood loss is less than or equal to 80 milliliters per cycle. Knowing these normal parameters is essential because deviations from them are what trigger further investigation."

---

## SLIDE 05 — HORMONE REVIEW
"Here is your hormone review. Gonadotropin-releasing hormone, or GnRH, comes from the hypothalamus and stimulates the pituitary. FSH, from the anterior pituitary, stimulates follicle maturation and is highest in the follicular phase. LH triggers ovulation through the LH surge and also supports the corpus luteum. Estrogen, produced by the ovarian follicle, proliferates the endometrium and makes cervical mucus thin and stretchy. Progesterone comes from the corpus luteum and creates a secretory endometrium. Prostaglandins from the endometrium drive uterine contractions and are the key player in dysmenorrhea pain. Know these six hormones, their sources, and their functions cold. You will be tested on them."

---

## SLIDE 06 — OVARIAN CYCLE
"The ovarian cycle has three phases. The follicular phase runs from day one to day fourteen. FSH stimulates follicle growth, estrogen rises, and the endometrium proliferates. Ovulation occurs on day fourteen — the LH surge causes the follicle to rupture and release a mature oocyte. Here is the critical point: ovulation always occurs fourteen days before the next menses begins, regardless of cycle length. The luteal phase runs from day fifteen to day twenty-eight. The corpus luteum secretes progesterone. If no fertilization occurs, the corpus luteum degenerates, hormones drop, and menstruation begins."

---

## SLIDE 07 — ENDOMETRIAL CYCLE
"Now the endometrial, or uterine, cycle. This runs in parallel with the ovarian cycle. The proliferative phase is estrogen-driven — the endometrium thickens. The secretory phase is progesterone-driven — glands secrete glycogen. The ischemic phase brings arteriole spasm and tissue ischemia if no implantation occurs. And finally, the menstrual phase: spiral arteries rupture, endometrial shedding occurs, and menses is the result. Notice how the ovarian cycle drives the endometrial cycle."

---

## SLIDE 08 — KEY CYCLE CONCEPTS
"Four key concepts to memorize. At ovulation, cervical mucus becomes thin, clear, and stretchy — this is called spinnbarkeit. At other times, it is thick and impenetrable. Basal body temperature rises half to one degree Fahrenheit after ovulation due to progesterone and remains elevated if pregnancy occurs. Mittelschmerz is mid-cycle unilateral pain at ovulation, experienced by about forty percent of ovulating individuals. Menarche occurs at an average age of 12.4 years, with a normal range of ten to sixteen. These are high-yield testing points."

---

## SLIDE 09 — DYSMENORRHEA OVERVIEW
"Moving to dysmenorrhea, which means painful menstruation. This condition affects fifty to ninety percent of menstruating individuals and is the leading cause of absenteeism related to gynecologic health. Risk factors include younger age, smoking, and stress. There are two categories: primary and secondary dysmenorrhea. The distinction between them is fundamental to nursing assessment and management."

---

## SLIDE 10 — PRIMARY vs SECONDARY
"Here is the comparison table. Primary dysmenorrhea is caused by increased prostaglandin production. It begins within a few years of menarche. The pain timing is one to two days before bleeding, peaking on days one and two. The pelvic exam is normal. Secondary dysmenorrhea is caused by underlying pelvic pathology — endometriosis is the most common cause. Onset is years after relatively painless periods. Pain worsens progressively over time. The pelvic exam may reveal masses or tenderness. The first-line treatment for primary is scheduled NSAIDs plus hormonal contraceptives. For secondary, you treat the underlying cause. If there is no improvement in three to six months, the next step is diagnostic laparoscopy."

---

## SLIDE 11 — TAKE NOTE: NSAIDs
"Take note of this critical point. Start NSAIDs before the pain becomes severe. They work by inhibiting prostaglandin synthesis. For maximum effect, begin one to two days before expected menses onset and continue on a scheduled basis — not PRN, not as needed — through days one and two of flow. Ibuprofen dosing is 400 to 800 milligrams every six to eight hours, with a maximum of 2,400 milligrams per day. Take it with food. If the patient gets no relief in three to six months, this warrants further investigation for secondary causes. Do not just keep refilling the NSAID prescription — you may be missing endometriosis or another underlying pathology."

---

## SLIDE 12 — MENOPAUSE DEFINITION
"Now we transition to menopause. Menopause is defined as one full year without a menstrual period. The average age is 51.4 years. Perimenopause is the transition period — it begins around age 47 and can last seven to fourteen years. This is a crucial counseling point: menopause is a natural maturing process. It is not a disease. More than 1.3 million people in the United States experience menopause each year."

---

## SLIDE 13 — SYSTEMIC EFFECTS PART 1
"Declining estrogen affects virtually every body system. In the central nervous system, patients experience hot flashes and night sweats — these are vasomotor symptoms from inappropriate peripheral vasodilation. About eighty percent are affected. They also have sleep disturbance, mood changes, and what patients often describe as brain fog. In the cardiovascular system, HDL decreases and cardiovascular disease risk goes up. CVD is the number one cause of death in females. The skeletal system loses bone density rapidly, leading to osteoporosis risk. And genitourinary syndrome of menopause, or GSM, involves vaginal dryness, dyspareunia, increased vaginal pH, and urinary symptoms. GSM is progressive and does not resolve without treatment."

---

## SLIDE 14 — SYSTEMIC EFFECTS PART 2
"The integumentary system shows dry, thin skin and decreased collagen leading to wrinkling. Body composition changes with increased abdominal fat and decreased lean muscle mass. Breast tissue loses density. For CVD, remember the female atypical presentation using the ABCDEFGH mnemonic: Angina or chest pain, Breathlessness, Chronic fatigue, Dizziness, Edema of hands and feet, Fluttering or palpitations, Gastric upset, and Heavy back or shoulder pain. Nurses — do not dismiss these symptoms as anxiety or indigestion. CVD is still often misperceived as a man's disease, which leads to delayed diagnosis in female patients."

---

## SLIDE 15 — MENOPAUSE MANAGEMENT
"Management of menopausal symptoms is individualized. For vasomotor symptoms, the gold standard is menopausal hormone therapy, or MHT, for moderate to severe cases. Non-hormonal options include SSRIs and SNRIs like venlafaxine and paroxetine, gabapentin, oxybutynin, and fezolinetant, which is a newer NK3 receptor antagonist approved in 2023. For genitourinary syndrome, use vaginal moisturizers and lubricants, low-dose vaginal estrogen, or ospemifene — an oral SERM. For osteoporosis prevention, calcium 1,200 milligrams, vitamin D 800 international units, weight-bearing exercise, and DEXA screening at baseline menopause. The MHT risk window is important: therapy is safest when started under age 60 or within ten years of menopause."

---

## SLIDE 16 — TAKE NOTE: CVD IN FEMALES
"Take note of this life-saving point. Cardiovascular disease is the leading cause of death in females. Female patients often present with atypical cardiac symptoms compared to males. The ABCDEFGH mnemonic is your tool. Do not dismiss fatigue, shortness of breath, or back pain as anxiety. Do not assume that a woman cannot be having a heart attack because she lacks crushing chest pain. This is a nursing priority that can literally save a life."

---

## SLIDE 17 — CONTRACEPTION OVERVIEW
"Ninety percent of reproductive-age females who do not want pregnancy use some form of contraception. The ideal method is safe, effective, easy to use, reversible, and culturally acceptable. Our role as nurses is to facilitate informed, shared decision-making. The best method is the one the patient uses consistently. The categories we will cover are behavioral methods, barrier methods, hormonal methods, long-acting reversible contraception, permanent methods, and emergency contraception."

---

## SLIDE 18 — BEHAVIORAL & BARRIER METHODS
"Starting with behavioral and barrier methods. Fertility awareness methods have a twenty-three percent typical-use failure rate — they require daily tracking, discipline, and regular cycles. Withdrawal has a twenty-two percent failure rate — pre-ejaculate may contain sperm. Lactational amenorrhea is only effective for the first six months postpartum with exclusive breastfeeding. The male condom has a thirteen percent failure rate and is the only method that protects against sexually transmitted infections, including HIV. The diaphragm has a seventeen percent failure rate, must be professionally fitted, and increases UTI risk."

---

## SLIDE 19 — HORMONAL METHODS PART 1
"Hormonal methods. Combined oral contraceptives have a seven percent failure rate. They suppress ovulation, thicken cervical mucus, and thin the endometrium. Contraindications include smoking over age 35, hypertension, migraines with aura, and history of DVT or PE. Progestin-only pills, or the mini-pill, also have a seven percent failure rate. They are safe for lactating patients and those with estrogen contraindications, but must be taken at the exact same time daily — within three hours. The transdermal patch and vaginal ring both have seven percent failure rates. Depo-Provera injections have a four percent failure rate but decrease bone mineral density with long-term use and may delay return to fertility up to eighteen months."

---

## SLIDE 20 — LARC & PERMANENT METHODS
"Now the most effective reversible methods. The etonogestrel implant, Nexplanon, has a failure rate of only zero-point-zero-five percent — that is five in ten thousand. The levonorgestrel IUD has a zero-point-two percent failure rate and lasts three to eight years. The copper IUD has a zero-point-eight percent failure rate and lasts up to ten years. It is also FDA-approved as emergency contraception. For permanent methods, tubal ligation has a zero-point-five percent failure rate, and vasectomy has a zero-point-fifteen percent failure rate. LARC methods — IUDs and implants — are the most effective reversible contraceptives available and require no daily adherence."

---

## SLIDE 21 — EMERGENCY CONTRACEPTION
"Emergency contraception. The copper IUD is the most effective method at over ninety-nine percent. It can be inserted up to five days after unprotected intercourse and provides ongoing long-term contraception. Ulipristal acetate, brand name ella, is available by prescription and effective up to five days. It is more effective than levonorgestrel, especially on days three through five. Levonorgestrel, Plan B One-Step, is over-the-counter with no age restriction, most effective within 72 hours but can be used up to one hundred twenty hours. It delays ovulation. It does not terminate an existing pregnancy. Counsel patients on this distinction."

---

## SLIDE 22 — TAKE NOTE: COUNSELING
"Take note of this contraceptive counseling pearl. Start every conversation with the question: what are your reproductive goals in the next year? For patients using estrogen-containing methods, teach the ACHES danger signs: Abdominal pain that is severe, Chest pain or shortness of breath, Headache that is severe, Eye problems or vision changes, and Severe leg pain — which could signal a DVT. For patients with an IUD, teach the PATCH mnemonic: Period late, Abdominal pain, Chills or fever, Trouble or foul discharge, and Heavy bleeding. Any of these signs means the patient should stop the method and seek immediate evaluation."

---

## SLIDE 23 — HIGH-YIELD CONTRACEPTION PEARLS
"Here are the high-yield pearls you will most likely see on an exam. Migraine with aura contraindicates estrogen — this is the key discriminator, not the word migraine alone. Migraine without aura is less restrictive. The copper IUD is the best emergency contraception. LARC methods offer the highest effectiveness without daily adherence. Typical-use failure rates reflect real-world inconsistent use — these are the numbers to counsel patients on. And only condoms provide STI protection. If you remember nothing else from the contraception section, remember these six points."

---

## SLIDE 24 — PRECONCEPTION OVERVIEW
"Now we shift to preconception care. The goal is to optimize maternal health for the best pregnancy outcomes. Preconception care should occur at any healthcare encounter with a person of childbearing potential — not just at a dedicated preconception visit. The period of greatest environmental sensitivity for the embryo is days 17 through 56 after conception, during organogenesis. This is often before the patient even knows they are pregnant. That is why preconception care and folic acid supplementation must begin before pregnancy, not after a positive test."

---

## SLIDE 25 — KEY INTERVENTIONS
"The key preconception interventions are these. Folic acid 400 to 800 micrograms daily for standard risk, or 4 milligrams daily if there is a prior neural tube defect, if the patient is on antiepileptics, or if they have pregestational diabetes. Immunizations: influenza annually, Tdap with each pregnancy, and rubella and varicella if not immune. For live vaccines, the patient must avoid pregnancy for one month after administration. Achieve a BMI in the normal range — obesity increases the risk of preeclampsia, GDM, cesarean, and stillbirth. Manage chronic conditions before conception by transitioning to pregnancy-safe medications. Review all medications and discontinue known teratogens. And screen for and treat STIs — untreated infections cause ectopic pregnancy, infertility, and PID."

---

## SLIDE 26 — RISK FACTORS TABLE
"This table summarizes the major risk factors for adverse pregnancy outcomes. Isotretinoin, brand name Accutane, causes cleft palate, congenital heart defects, and intellectual disability. Alcohol causes fetal alcohol spectrum disorder — there is no safe amount and no safe trimester. Valproic acid causes neural tube defects and cognitive deficits. Uncontrolled diabetes increases birth defects and stillbirth. Folic acid deficiency is directly linked to neural tube defects, which supplementation reduces by up to seventy percent. Obesity is associated with preeclampsia, GDM, cesarean birth, and stillbirth."

---

## SLIDE 27 — INHERITANCE PATTERNS PART 1
"Genetic counseling begins with understanding inheritance patterns. Autosomal dominant disorders require only one mutant gene. An affected person usually has one affected parent. Each offspring has a fifty percent chance of inheriting the disorder. Examples include Huntington disease, Marfan syndrome, and neurofibromatosis. Autosomal recessive requires two copies of the mutant gene. Both parents are obligate carriers and are clinically unaffected. Each pregnancy has a twenty-five percent chance of an affected child, a fifty percent chance of a carrier, and a twenty-five percent chance of an unaffected non-carrier. Examples include cystic fibrosis, sickle cell disease, and Tay-Sachs disease. X-linked recessive predominantly affects males. There is no male-to-male transmission. A carrier mother has a fifty percent chance of affected sons and a fifty percent chance of carrier daughters. Examples include hemophilia A and B and Duchenne muscular dystrophy."

---

## SLIDE 28 — INHERITANCE PATTERNS PART 2
"X-linked dominant disorders show a different pattern. An affected male will pass the condition to all of his daughters and none of his sons. An affected female has a fifty percent chance of passing it to each offspring regardless of sex. Males are usually more severely affected, and many X-linked dominant disorders are lethal in males. Examples include Rett syndrome. Multifactorial disorders involve multiple genes plus environmental factors. The recurrence risk is lower, typically two to five percent, and varies by condition and family history. Examples include cleft lip and palate and spina bifida. One specific population screening note: for patients of Ashkenazi Jewish descent, prioritize carrier screening for cystic fibrosis, where one in twenty-nine are carriers, and Tay-Sachs disease, where one in thirty are carriers."

---

## SLIDE 29 — TAKE NOTE: GENETIC COUNSELING
"Take note of this critical point about genetic counseling. The ideal time is before conception. Preconception carrier screening allows couples to understand their risks, explore reproductive options, and plan for early prenatal diagnosis. The Genetic Information Nondiscrimination Act of 2008, known as GINA, protects against discrimination by health insurers and employers but does not extend to life insurance, disability insurance, or long-term care insurance. As nurses, we provide nondirective, nonjudgmental information and support. The ultimate reproductive decision rests solely with the patient or couple. We do not impose our personal beliefs."

---

## SLIDE 30 — KEY TERMS CONTRACEPTION
"Let me quickly review key terminology for the contraception section. COC stands for Combined Oral Contraceptive. POP is Progestin-Only Pill. LARC is Long-Acting Reversible Contraception, which includes IUDs and implants. DMPA is depot medroxyprogesterone acetate, the injectable given every thirteen weeks. LNG-IUD is the levonorgestrel-releasing intrauterine device. Cu-IUD is the copper IUD. EC is emergency contraception. VTE is venous thromboembolism, the key risk of estrogen-containing contraceptives. You will see these abbreviations throughout the course and on the NCLEX."

---

## SLIDE 31 — KEY TERMS MENOPAUSE
"For the menopause and gynecology section, know these terms. VMS stands for vasomotor symptoms — hot flashes and night sweats. GSM is genitourinary syndrome of menopause, involving vaginal and urinary changes. MHT is menopausal hormone therapy. POI is primary ovarian insufficiency, which is ovarian failure before age 40. AUB is abnormal uterine bleeding. Primary dysmenorrhea means painful menses with no pelvic pathology. Secondary dysmenorrhea means painful menses caused by an identifiable disease process, most commonly endometriosis."

## SLIDE 32 — NGN CASE: ADOLESCENT
"Let us apply what we have learned with an NGN-style unfolding case. Jordan is sixteen years old, sexually active, and requesting contraception at a school-based clinic. She reports occasional migraine with aura. She is a nonsmoker and wants a method she does not have to take every day. What are the key cues here? Number one: migraine with aura. This is the estrogen eligibility concern — it raises her stroke risk with combined hormonal methods. Number two: her stated preference for a non-daily method. This points us toward LARC. Number three: the need for dual protection. Any method we choose must be paired with condom teaching for STI prevention."

---

## SLIDE 33 — CASE: METHOD ELIGIBILITY
"Analyzing method eligibility for Jordan. Combined oral contraceptives should be avoided because the estrogen in them increases stroke risk when the patient has migraine with aura. The levonorgestrel IUD is appropriate — it is progestin-only and lasts for years. The etonogestrel implant is appropriate for the same reasons. Progestin-only pills are appropriate but they require daily adherence, which she specifically wants to avoid. The copper IUD is appropriate — it is non-hormonal and lasts ten years. Our priority is to discuss LARC options because they match her preference for a non-daily method while avoiding the estrogen contraindication."

---

## SLIDE 34 — CASE: NURSING ACTIONS
"The nursing actions in this case are clear. First, present LARC and progestin-only options with their typical-use effectiveness rates so she can make an informed choice. Second, provide condom teaching for STI prevention regardless of the method she chooses. Third, teach the danger signs specific to whichever method she selects — ACHES or PATCH. Fourth, document the shared decision. Fifth, offer STI screening as part of comprehensive care. We evaluate the outcome by continuation of the method, tolerability, her satisfaction, and whether she maintains STI protection over time — not just at a single visit."

## SLIDE 49 — CASE DEBRIEF
"Let me leave you with the three points students most often miss on the adolescent contraception case. Number one: overlooking aura. The word migraine alone is not the contraindication.
