## INF_1523_Day1_01_Menstrual_Cycle.png

**Alt text:** "Infographic of the menstrual cycle showing the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian axis. A circular 28-day cycle diagram is divided into the follicular phase (days 1–14), ovulation (day 14), and the luteal phase (days 15–28). Hormone panels show FSH rising in the follicular phase, an LH surge triggering ovulation, estrogen peaking before ovulation, and progesterone dominating the luteal phase. The endometrial cycle is shown below with proliferative, secretory, ischemic, and menstrual phases aligned to the ovarian cycle. Three callout boxes explain cervical mucus changes (thin and stretchy at ovulation), basal body temperature rise after ovulation, and mittelschmerz mid-cycle pain."

**Plain-text companion summary:** The menstrual cycle is regulated by the hypothalamic–pituitary–ovarian axis. **Ovarian cycle:** Follicular phase (days 1–14, FSH-driven, estrogen rises), Ovulation (day 14, LH surge), Luteal phase (days 15–28, progesterone from corpus luteum). **Endometrial cycle:** Proliferative phase (estrogen thickens lining), Secretory phase (progesterone prepares for implantation), Ischemic phase (vasospasm if no implantation), Menstrual phase (shedding). **Key hormones:** GnRH (hypothalamus) → FSH + LH (anterior pituitary) → Estrogen + Progesterone (ovaries). **Cycle signs:** Cervical mucus becomes thin, clear, and stretchy (spinnbarkeit) at ovulation; BBT rises 0.5–1°F after ovulation; mittelschmerz = unilateral ovulatory pain in ~40% of ovulators. Normal cycle: 21–35 days; bleeding ≤8 days; average blood loss ≤80 mL.

## INF_1523_Day1_02_Dysmenorrhea.png

**Alt text:** "A two-column comparison infographic contrasting primary and secondary dysmenorrhea. The left column (blue header, 'Primary / Spasmodic') shows increased prostaglandin production causing uterine contraction and vasoconstriction, onset within a few years of menarche, normal pelvic exam, suprapubic cramping peaking on days 1–2, and first-line treatment with scheduled NSAIDs plus hormonal contraceptives. The right column (red header, 'Secondary / Congestive') shows underlying pelvic pathology including endometriosis implants, fibroids, and PID, onset years after painless periods, abnormal pelvic exam, associated symptoms of dyspareunia and infertility, and treatment addressing the root cause. A central 'Take Note!' banner emphasizes starting NSAIDs 1–2 days BEFORE menses onset, on a scheduled basis."

**Plain-text companion summary:** **Primary dysmenorrhea:** Caused by increased prostaglandin production in an ovulatory cycle → uterine hypercontractility and vasoconstriction. Begins within a few years of menarche. Normal pelvic exam. Pain = suprapubic cramping, peaks days 1–2; may include nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, headache, fatigue. Treatment: scheduled NSAIDs (ibuprofen 400–800 mg q6–8h or naproxen 250–550 mg q6–8h) started 1–2 days before menses + hormonal contraceptives. Non-pharmacologic: heat, exercise, yoga, acupuncture. **Secondary dysmenorrhea:** Caused by underlying pelvic pathology — endometriosis (#1 cause), adenomyosis, fibroids, PID, cervical stenosis, IUD. Onset later in life after years of relatively painless periods. Abnormal pelvic exam. Associated with dyspareunia, heavy/irregular bleeding, infertility, chronic pelvic pain. Treatment: address underlying cause (laparoscopy, GnRH agonists, surgery) + NSAIDs/hormones. If no improvement in 3–6 months → diagnostic laparoscopy.

## INF_1523_Day1_03_Menopause.png

**Alt text:** "Infographic on menopausal transition and menopause. A central timeline shows perimenopause beginning around age 47 (7–14 years of irregular cycles) leading to menopause at age 51.4 (1 full year without menses). Seven body-system panels radiate outward, each color-coded: brain/CNS (hot flashes, night sweats, mood changes, brain fog), cardiovascular (↓ HDL, ↑ CVD risk, atypical female MI signs ABCDEFGH), skeletal (bone loss → osteoporosis, 1 in 2 females >50 fracture), genitourinary (GSM: vaginal dryness, dyspareunia, urinary symptoms), integumentary (dry thin skin, ↓ collagen), body composition (↑ abdominal fat, ↓ lean muscle), and breasts (tenderness, tissue changes). **Plain-text companion summary:** **Menopause:** Permanent cessation of menses, defined as 1 year without a menstrual period. Average age: 51.4 years (range 47–55). **Perimenopause:** Transition from onset of irregular cycles to final menses; begins ~age 47, lasts 7–14 years. The decline in estrogen affects every body system. **CNS:** Hot flashes/night sweats (vasomotor instability, ~80% affected), sleep disturbance, mood changes, "brain fog." **Cardiovascular:** ↓ HDL, ↑ CVD risk (#1 cause of death in females); atypical MI signs = ABCDEFGH (Angina, Breathlessness, Chronic fatigue, Dizziness, Edema, Fluttering, Gastric upset, Heavy back/shoulder pain). **Skeletal:** Rapid bone loss → osteoporosis; 1 in 2 females >50 will fracture. **Genitourinary:** GSM — vaginal dryness, dyspareunia, ↑ pH → ↑ infections, urinary urgency/frequency. **Management:** MHT is gold standard for moderate-severe vasomotor symptoms in healthy patients <60 years and <10 years from menopause. Non-hormonal: SSRIs/SNRIs, gabapentin, fezolinetant (NK3R antagonist). Osteoporosis prevention: calcium 1,200 mg/day, vitamin D 800 IU/day, weight-bearing exercise, DEXA screening. GSM: vaginal estrogen, moisturizers, lubricants (progressive — does not self-resolve).

## INF_1523_Day1_04_Contraceptive_Methods.png

**Alt text:** "A comprehensive contraceptive methods grid infographic organized into four color-coded categories. Behavioral methods (green): fertility awareness, withdrawal, and LAM — each showing a calendar icon and mechanism. Barrier methods (blue): male and female condoms, diaphragm, cervical cap, and sponge — each with a physical barrier icon and typical-use failure rates. Hormonal methods (purple): combined OCs, progestin-only pills, patch, ring, injectable, implant, LNG-IUD, and copper IUD — each with mechanism icons (suppress ovulation, thicken mucus) and failure rates. Permanent methods (red): tubal ligation and vasectomy. A bottom panel highlights emergency contraception options (levonorgestrel OTC, ulipristal acetate Rx, copper IUD). A callout box lists the ACHES danger signs for estrogen-containing methods."

**Plain-text companion summary:** 90% of U.S. females of reproductive age not wanting pregnancy use contraception. **Behavioral:** Fertility awareness (23% failure), withdrawal (22%), LAM (0.45–7.5%, only effective first 6 months postpartum if exclusively breastfeeding and amenorrheic). **Barrier:** Male condom (13%) — only method that protects against STIs; female condom (21%); diaphragm (17%) — must be fitted; cervical cap (17–23%) — higher failure in parous; sponge (14% nulliparous, 27% parous) — OTC. **Hormonal:** Combined OCs (7%) — contraindicated if smoking >35, HTN, migraines with aura, Hx DVT/PE; progestin-only pills (7%) — safe for lactation, must take same time daily; patch/ring (7%); Depo-Provera injection (4%) — ↓ bone density with long-term use; Nexplanon implant (0.05%) — most effective reversible method; LNG-IUD (0.2%) — lasts 3–8 years; Copper IUD (0.8%) — non-hormonal, lasts 10 years, also EC. **Permanent:** Tubal ligation (0.5%); vasectomy (0.15%) — needs confirmation of azoospermia. **EC:** Levonorgestrel OTC ≤72 h; ulipristal acetate Rx ≤120 h; copper IUD >99% effective ≤5 days. **Teach ACHES:** Abdominal pain, Chest pain/SOB, Headache severe, Eye problems, Severe leg pain.

## INF_1523_Day1_05_Hormonal_vs_Barrier_vs_LARC.png

**Alt text:** "A three-panel comparison infographic analyzing contraceptive methods by mechanism of action. The left panel (blue, 'Hormonal Methods') shows an ovary and pituitary with suppression arrows: combined OCs, patch, and ring suppress ovulation via negative feedback on FSH/LH; progestin-only methods (mini-pill, injection, implant) thicken cervical mucus and thin endometrium. The center panel (green, 'Barrier Methods') shows physical blockade: condoms, diaphragm, cap, and sponge blocking sperm from reaching the cervix; spermicide disabling sperm. The right panel (orange, 'Long-Acting Reversible Contraception — LARC') shows an IUD in the uterus and an arm implant — 'fit and forget' methods with <1% failure, no daily adherence required, and immediate return to fertility upon removal. A bottom strip compares typical-use failure rates: LARC <1%, hormonal ~7%, barrier ~13–21%."

**Plain-text companion summary:** **Hormonal methods** work by suppressing ovulation (estrogen + progestin combinations suppress FSH/LH), thickening cervical mucus (progestin-only), and thinning the endometrium. Require daily/weekly/monthly adherence. Typical-use failure ~4–7%. Contraindications include smoking >35, HTN, migraines with aura, history of DVT/PE (for estrogen-containing methods). **Barrier methods** physically block sperm from reaching the cervix. Male condom is the only method that also protects against STIs. Typical-use failure 13–27%. Require correct use with every act of intercourse. **LARC (Long-Acting Reversible Contraception):** IUDs (LNG 0.2% failure, Copper 0.8%) and implant (Nexplanon 0.05%) are the most effective reversible methods. Benefits: no daily adherence, cost-effective over time, rapid return to fertility upon removal. IUDs can be used for emergency contraception (copper). LARC is recommended as first-line for adolescents and nulliparous patients by ACOG and AAP.

## INF_1523_Day1_06_Natural_Family_Planning.png

**Alt text:** "Infographic on fertility awareness-based methods (natural family planning). A 28-day circular cycle shows the fertile window (days 9–17 highlighted). Four method panels: (1) Basal Body Temperature — a thermometer graphic with an ovulation temperature spike of 0.5–1°F sustained through the luteal phase; (2) Cervical Mucus / Billings Ovulation Method — illustrations of mucus changing from thick/cloudy to thin/clear/stretchy (spinnbarkeit) at ovulation; (3) Standard Days Method — a calendar with CycleBeads showing fertile days 8–19; (4) Symptothermal Method — combining BBT, cervical mucus, and calendar tracking. A 'Two-Day Method' callout box explains checking for cervical secretions daily. Bottom panel shows a comparison of typical-use failure rate (23%) vs perfect-use rate (<5%), and lists advantages (no hormones, no devices, acceptable to all religions) and disadvantages (requires discipline, irregular cycles reduce reliability, no STI protection)."

**Plain-text companion summary:** Fertility awareness-based methods (FABMs) rely on identifying the fertile window and avoiding intercourse or using barrier methods during that time. **Basal Body Temperature (BBT):** Take temperature daily before arising; temperature rises 0.5–1°F after ovulation due to progesterone and stays elevated until next menses. Identifies ovulation retrospectively — confirms it occurred, but does not predict it in advance. **Cervical Mucus / Billings Ovulation Method:** Monitor cervical mucus daily. At ovulation, mucus becomes thin, clear, slippery, and stretchy (spinnbarkeit) — like raw egg white — indicating peak fertility. After ovulation, mucus becomes thick, cloudy, and tacky. **Standard Days Method (SDM):** For cycles 26–32 days; use CycleBeads to track fertile days 8–19. **Symptothermal Method:** Combines BBT + cervical mucus + calendar tracking for highest effectiveness among FABMs. **Lactational Amenorrhea Method (LAM):** Exclusive breastfeeding suppresses ovulation; only effective for first 6 months postpartum if amenorrheic and exclusively breastfeeding (no supplements). **Typical-use failure rate:** 23%; perfect-use failure rate: <5%. No side effects, no cost, acceptable to most religions — but requires daily commitment, regular cycles, and partner cooperation.

## INF_1523_Day1_07_Preconception_Genetic_Counseling.png

**Alt text:** "Infographic on preconception care and genetic counseling. A central 'Preconception Optimization' hub connects to ten intervention spokes: folic acid 400–800 mcg/day, immunizations (influenza, Tdap, rubella, varicella, hepatitis B), healthy BMI (18.5–24.9), chronic disease management (diabetes HbA1c <6.5%, HTN, thyroid, seizure disorders), medication review (identify teratogens), STI screening, substance cessation (alcohol, smoking, drugs), IPV screening, environmental exposure assessment, and reproductive life plan. A right-side panel titled 'Who Needs Genetic Counseling?' lists eight indications: maternal age ≥35, paternal age ≥40, prior child with genetic disorder, ≥2 pregnancy losses, abnormal prenatal screening, consanguinity, known carrier status, and ethnicity-based risk (sickle cell — African; Tay–Sachs — Ashkenazi Jewish; thalassemia — Mediterranean; cystic fibrosis — Northern European). A bottom panel shows the five inheritance patterns — autosomal dominant, autosomal recessive, X-linked recessive, X-linked dominant, and multifactorial — each with a representative disorder icon and recurrence risk. A 'Take Note!' box: 'Ideal time for genetic counseling is BEFORE conception.'"

**Plain-text companion summary:** **Preconception care** aims to identify and modify biomedical, behavioral, and social risks before pregnancy to optimize outcomes. The period of greatest embryonic sensitivity is days 17–56 after conception (organogenesis) — often before pregnancy is recognized. **Key interventions:** (1) Folic acid 400–800 mcg/day (4 mg if prior NTD, antiepileptics, or diabetes) — reduces NTDs by two-thirds. (2) Update immunizations. (3) Achieve BMI 18.5–24.9. (4) Optimize chronic conditions. (5) Review all medications for teratogenicity (isotretinoin, valproic acid, warfarin, ACE inhibitors). (6) Screen and treat STIs. (7) Stop smoking, alcohol, illicit drugs — no safe level. (8) Screen for intimate partner violence. (9) Assess environmental/occupational exposures. (10) Develop a reproductive life plan: "Would you like to become pregnant in the next year?" **Genetic counseling indications:** maternal age ≥35, paternal age ≥40, prior affected child, ≥2 pregnancy losses, abnormal screening, consanguinity, known carrier status, ethnicity-based risk. **Inheritance patterns:** Autosomal dominant (50% offspring, e.g., Huntington, Marfan), autosomal recessive (25% affected, e.g., CF, sickle cell, Tay–Sachs), X-linked recessive (males affected, e.g., hemophilia, Duchenne MD), X-linked dominant (rare, e.g., hypophosphatemic rickets), multifactorial (genes + environment, e.g., cleft lip/palate, spina bifida). GINA 2008 protects against genetic discrimination by insurers and employers but not life/disability/LTC insurance.
